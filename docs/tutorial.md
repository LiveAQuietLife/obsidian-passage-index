# Follow a passage through your notes

Open a note and see where else you've studied the same verses. Learn Passage Index by following the clouds, the piercing, and the mourning from Revelation 1:7–8.

**Why this passage?** Revelation 1:7 is two Old Testament texts in one verse:

> “Behold, He is coming with the clouds, and every eye will see Him—even those who pierced Him. And all the tribes of the earth will mourn because of Him.” (Rev 1:7, BSB)

The clouds come from Daniel 7:13; the piercing and the mourning from Zechariah 12:10. Jesus had already put those two texts together (Matthew 24:30), and John's Gospel quotes Zechariah at the cross (John 19:37). Study this one verse and you're soon working in five other passages. Keeping track of what you've written on each, and how they connect, is the job Passage Index does.

---

## Setup: Open the demo vault

1. Download the demo vault and extract it. In Obsidian desktop, choose **Open folder as vault** and select the extracted `demo-vault` folder.
2. When Obsidian asks whether you trust the vault's author, choose **Trust author and enable plugins** if you trust the source. If the pane command is missing, open **Settings → Community plugins** and check that Passage Index is enabled.
3. Open `Start here`. Press **Ctrl + P** (**Cmd + P** on a Mac), run **Passage Index: Open pane**, and select the **Index** tab.
4. **Previews need Ctrl.** Hold **Ctrl** (**Cmd** on a Mac) and point at a result in the pane to preview that note. A plain point shows what a tag means instead. If you'd rather preview without the key, open **Settings → Page preview** (under *Core plugins*) and turn off **Passage Index**. (Passage Index's own settings, **Pane → Hover previews**, has a button that opens the same page.)

> **Using your own vault?** Try the demo first. Then install Passage Index from **Settings → Community plugins → Browse**. It needs Obsidian desktop 1.5.0 or later.

## 1. Find notes that share a verse

**Why use it.** When you study Revelation 1:7, the first question is: *what have I already written about Daniel 7:13 and Zechariah 12:10?* Searching for "Daniel" finds too much, and searching for "Dan 7:13" misses the note where you wrote "Daniel 7.13". Passage Index reads every reference in every note, in whatever form you wrote it, and lists the notes beside the one you're reading, grouped by passage.

Open `Passages/Revelation 1v7-8`. Passage Index reads the filename as **Revelation 1:7–8**, the note's *main passage*, and below it makes one group for each passage the note cites.

1. Expand **Daniel 7:13**. `Daniel 7v13-14` comes first, marked **about**: its main passage includes the verse, so it's the note *on* Daniel 7:13. Below it are notes that only cite the verse, tagged **same verse**, among them `Matthew 24v29-31` and `Mark 14v61-62`. (*Same verse* describes the reference, not the wording: the note cites Daniel 7:13, it doesn't necessarily quote it. Point at a tag to read it as a sentence.)
2. Open `Daniel 7v13-14`. The pane now follows the Daniel note. Use the **trail** above the tabs to step back to `Revelation 1v7-8`.
3. Expand **Zechariah 12:10** and open `Zechariah 12v10`. From there, expand **John 19:37** and open `John 19v31-37`.
4. To read a passage without leaving the pane, hold **Ctrl** (**Cmd** on a Mac) and point at its heading. To read the note's own passage, click it at the top of the pane. The demo has the BSB loaded.

**What it shows about the text.** Look at who turns up under Daniel 7:13: Matthew, Mark and Revelation. The Son of Man coming with the clouds isn't a one-off image; three New Testament writers reach for it. And under Zechariah 12:10, John's Gospel and Revelation both use the same verse, one looking back at the cross, the other ahead to Christ's coming. The groups have already shown you the shape of the study.

## 2. See which passages travel together

**Why use it.** The Index answers *who cites this verse?* **Connections** answers a different question: *which passages do my notes keep citing alongside it?* That's how you spot a theme: verses that keep turning up in each other's company across your notes.

1. In `Revelation 1v7-8`, open the pane's **Map** tab. Click chapter **1**, then verse **7**. (Or run **Passage Index: Open connections** for a larger view.)
2. Below the verse grid is Connections: Revelation 1:7 in the centre, the passages your notes cite with it around it. Old Testament on the left, New Testament on the right; thicker lines mean more notes make the link. By default it shows passages cited by **2 or more** of these notes.
3. Click **Matthew 24:30** to put it in the centre, then look again.

**What it shows about the text.** Daniel 7:13 and Zechariah 12:10 sit next to Revelation 1:7, which you expected. But Matthew 24:30 is there with a thick line too, and when you put it in the centre, Daniel and Zechariah are beside it as well. The pairing of the clouds and the mourning didn't start with John: Jesus made it first. Mark 14:62 (Jesus before the high priest) and Revelation 14:14 (one like a son of man on a cloud) are the next places the thread goes.

**What counts as a connection.** A short note, or one about the centre verse, counts as a whole. In a longer note, only what's cited in the same paragraph as the centre verse, or under a heading for that verse, counts. That keeps a long note's passing references from cluttering the picture.

## 3. Record how two passages connect

**Why use it.** A shared reference tells you two notes mention the same verse. It doesn't say that Revelation 1:7 *uses* Daniel 7:13. An **intertext** records that relationship, and the kind of use:

| Kind | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **quotes** | The words are carried over, close to exactly, usually as Scripture | John 19:37 quotes Zechariah 12:10: “They will look on the One they have pierced.” |
| **alludes to** | A distinctive phrase or a few key words, meant to be recognised | Revelation 1:7 alludes to Daniel 7:13: “coming with the clouds” |
| **echoes** | A fainter link: a word, an image, maybe not deliberate | Matthew 24:29's darkened sun echoes Isaiah 13:10 |

You record it once, in either note, and both notes show it. That's the payoff: open the Old Testament note and see every New Testament text that uses it.

1. In `Revelation 1v7-8`, expand **Intertexts**. Under **This passage uses** are Daniel 7:13 and Zechariah 12:10, both *alluded to*.
2. Open `Zechariah 12v10` and expand its **Intertexts**. Under **Used by** are John 19:31–37 (*quotes*), Matthew 24:29–31 and Revelation 1:7–8 (*alludes to*). The Zechariah note never had to list them.
3. Back in `Revelation 1v7-8`, look at the end of the Intertexts section: **Cited here, not yet intertexts**. These are passages the note cites but hasn't recorded: **Matthew 24:30** and **John 19:37**.

**Now decide.** This is where the text matters, not the tool.

- **Matthew 24:30.** Revelation 1:7 and Matthew 24:30 combine the same two Old Testament texts, and both widen Zechariah's mourners (the families of Jerusalem) to "all the tribes of the earth." Did John know Jesus' saying, or did both draw on Daniel and Zechariah on their own? If you think Revelation is echoing Jesus, click **Matthew 24:30**, choose **echoes**, check the sentence the window shows (*Revelation 1:7–8 echoes Matthew 24:30*), and select **Add**. If not, leave it.
- **John 19:37.** John's Gospel and Revelation both use Zechariah 12:10, but neither is using the other. That relationship is already recorded through Zechariah. Leave it.

The list only suggests; nothing is recorded until you choose **Add**. You can also type the line yourself under the `## Intertexts` heading:

```
- Matt 24:30 (echoes) — the clouds and the mourning together
```

Use **quoted by**, **alluded to by** or **echoed by** when the other passage is the one doing the using. One passage per line.

## 4. Save a question for later

**Why use it.** Study opens more doors than you can walk through in one sitting. In the Daniel note you'll notice Revelation 14:14: *“seated on the cloud was One like the Son of Man.”* That's worth a study of its own, but not now. A **rabbit trail** saves the passage and your reason, and brings it back whenever you're in a note that touches it.

1. Open the **Rabbit trails** tab. The demo already has one: Revelation 14:14, saved from `Daniel 7v13-14`.
2. Add your own. In `Revelation 1v7-8`'s Index, right-click **Zechariah 12:10** and choose **Save as a rabbit trail…**. For the reason, write: *How does the mourning in Zechariah compare with Revelation's?* Select **Save**.
3. The entries live in an ordinary note, `Rabbit trails.md`. Delete a line when you're done with it, or right-click it in the tab and choose **Remove (done with it)…**.

For a question about *how* two passages relate, use an `#intertext` task instead. Open `John 19v31-37`: its question asks whether John 19:36 (“not one of His bones will be broken”) comes from Exodus 12:46, the Passover lamb, or Psalm 34:20. When you write a note on either passage, the question shows up there under **Open questions**.

## 5. Pick what to study next

**Why use it.** After a while your notes cite many passages you've never studied on their own. **Gaps** lists them, most-cited first. The top of the list is where your study keeps pointing.

1. Open the **Gaps** tab and choose **Revelation** in the book filter.
2. At the top are **Revelation 1:13**, **Revelation 14:14** and **Revelation 21:6**, each cited by three notes. Revelation 14:14 is tagged **rabbit trail**: it's the verse you saved for later. Expand it to see which notes cite it.
3. Choose **Start a note**. Check the name (`Revelation 14v14`) and the folder, and select **Create**. The new note begins with a line showing where you came from, and the gap disappears.

**What it shows about the text.** Two of the three passages at the top are Revelation's own "one like a son of man": among the lampstands in 1:13, and seated on a cloud with a sickle for the harvest in 14:14. The thread that started with Daniel's son of man coming with the clouds runs on through the book, and Gaps found it because your notes kept citing it. The **Map** tab shows the same thing as a grid: verses with notes are shaded, so you can see at a glance which parts of a book you've covered.

## Quick reference

| You want to… | Use | Where |
|---|---|---|
| See every note citing the passages in this note | Passage groups | **Index** tab |
| Know which note is *about* a verse | The **about** tag | Index tab, in a group |
| Read a verse | Ctrl/Cmd + point at a passage heading; click the main passage at the top | Pane |
| Get back to where you started | The trail | Above the tabs |
| See what your notes cite alongside a verse | **Connections** | **Map** tab, pick a verse; or **Open connections** |
| Record that one passage uses another | **Add intertext…**, or click a passage under *Cited here* | **Intertexts** section, or right-click a reference |
| Keep a passage for later | **Save as a rabbit trail…** | Right-click a passage; **Rabbit trails** tab |
| Find passages you cite but haven't studied | **Gaps**, then **Start a note** | **Gaps** tab |
| See which parts of a book you've covered | The verse grid | **Map** tab |
